# Chapter 1: The Lamp

Aldous counted the stairs the way other men counted prayers. Forty-one to the
lamp room, iron cold under his palm, each step answering the last. He did not need
to count. His feet knew the climb. But counting was a kind of company, and he had
learned to keep what company he could.

At the top the glass was furred with salt. He wiped a clean arc with his sleeve and
looked out at nothing: a flat dark sea, no running lights, no ships. There had not
been a ship in a long time. The harbor down the coast had grown a deeper channel,
and the channel had taken the traffic, and the traffic had never come back. Still
the lamp turned. Still he climbed.

He trimmed the wick first, as he always did, thumb steady against the brass. The
oil smelled the way it had smelled for thirty years, sweet and faintly burnt, and
the smell did what it always did, which was to put the years back in order. He set
the flame and watched it catch and hold. Outside, the beam swung once across the
black water and found no one, and swung again.

On the sill by the window stood two cups. One was his, chipped at the rim, still
warm. The other was clean and dry and empty, set out from habit his hands obeyed
before his mind could stop them. He looked at it for a moment. Then he looked away,
because looking was its own small surrender, and he had work yet to do.

He wound the mechanism, the weights descending slow as patience, and the great lens
went on turning. The light was for no ship now. He knew that. He had known it for
years. But a light is a promise a man makes to the dark, and a promise does not ask
whether anyone is left to keep it for.

When the flame was steady he sat by the window with his warm cup and the empty one,
and he watched the beam go out and out across the water until the first grey
loosened the edge of the sky. Then he rose, and banked the lamp for the day, and
began, without hurry, the forty-one steps down.
